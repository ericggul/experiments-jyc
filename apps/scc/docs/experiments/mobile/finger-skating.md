# Mobile finger-skating/default — three vector-field options

- **Route:** `/mobile/finger-skating/default` (was `/1` until 2026-10-07; `/1` redirects)
- **Date:** 2026-09-25
- **Reference:** `finger-skating/field/1`'s captured-pointer discipline. This is a separate, single-device directional field.

The entire viewport is a uniformly spaced grid of directional arrows. At common phone portrait sizes, the grid contains more than 1,000 arrows (roughly 1,150); spacing, row count, and column count respond to viewport size. Desktop keeps equal spacing in both axes and a comparable minimum count. The bottom-right control switches between `opt 1`, `opt 2`, and `opt 3`; `opt 2` opens by default. All canvases remain mounted so their accumulated fields survive switching.

## Opt 1 — preserved direct alignment

Before touch, a uniform rightward field gives every arrow a defined direction. Pointer-down captures the gesture; each movement segment drives the nearby arrows toward its travel direction. An arrow's turn depends on its shortest distance `d` to that segment: the influence is `(1 − (d/R)²)³` inside radius `R`, and zero outside. An arrow directly on the path points in the finger's direction; farther arrows turn progressively less. There is no flat fully aligned disk. The radius remains at the previous pixel range, calculated independently of arrow density (roughly 60–80 px on common phones, 96 px at 1440 × 900). Within one gesture, a cell uses its closest path approach as the influence strength, so overlapping pointer samples do not cumulatively flatten the radial falloff. Direction persists after release; a later gesture can redirect the same region. This is a locally driven orientation field, not an electrostatic potential or fluid simulation. Arrow length stays constant so the changed orientation remains legible.

The field is drawn on one canvas when a segment changes directions or the viewport changes. Segment-to-cell distance is evaluated only in the path's local bounding area; changed cells are collected across coalesced pointer samples and repainted once per animation frame. This avoids dense point stamping and responds even to short movements. The full grid is drawn only on first load, resize, or a large changed area. Pointer bounds are read once per input event, and canvas DPR is capped at 1.5. Resize interpolates the existing direction field instead of resetting it.

## Opt 2 — persistent dipole flow

The question here is whether a swipe can change a *field* rather than directly pose each arrow. An electric point charge gives radial field structure; a magnetic current gives circulation, but neither means that a moving finger literally creates those fields. A 2D incompressible flow's streamfunction offers a closer visual and mechanical analogy: its perpendicular gradient produces a continuous velocity field with forward transport and circulation around it. These distinctions follow [MIT's electric-field notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/408d683395106a54b6d89433457abb6c_chap2coulomb_law.pdf) and [streamfunction-vorticity lecture](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-29-numerical-fluid-mechanics-spring-2015/765313c75c640191ca68e174f5296720_MIT2_29S15_Lecture19.pdf).

Each short portion of finger travel deposits a compact streamfunction dipole, `ψ = A n(1 − r²/R²)⁴` for `r < R`, in travel-aligned coordinates (`n` is the perpendicular offset). Its perpendicular gradient points with the finger on the central path, bends around the approaching/leaving sides, and creates a weaker returning flow farther to either side. Each deposit is proportional to traveled distance, so pointer sampling frequency does not set the strength. Contributions add into a persistent target field; velocity is capped to keep repeated strokes bounded. A damped second-order response lets arrows accelerate, overshoot, and settle into that changed field rather than snapping or resetting. A later gesture changes it again. This is a deliberately local, persistent artistic flow model, not a full Navier–Stokes solver or an electromagnetic simulation. [Stam's Stable Fluids paper](https://www.josstam.com/publications) is a reference for the distinction between a dynamic fluid solver and this lighter field instrument.

Opt 2 arrows encode both direction and approximate magnitude through angle and length. [MIT's vector notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-02sc-multivariable-calculus-fall-2010/38223d6ec70b12de9d01446438100b9d_MIT18_02SC_notes_0.pdf) give that standard arrow convention. A radius near 98 px on a 390 × 844 viewport keeps the response local. Only path-near cells receive input, and only unsettled cells animate; the animation stops once they settle. One canvas per option draws changed cells once per frame, with full redraw only on load, resize, or a large changed area. DPR remains capped at 1.5. Resize interpolates the persistent field. Model tests cover central forward flow, side return, distant invariance, settling, subsequent gesture, and resize. Browser interaction and frame timing remain unverified pending explicit runtime authorization.

## Opt 3 — vortex sheet in a still layer (2026-10-02)

Opt 1 and opt 2 still write arrows locally: the finger either poses each arrow or stamps a target vector beside its path, and nothing beyond the stamp changes. Opt 3 changes the *field's sources* instead and lets the field follow from them everywhere.

The screen is a window onto a thin 2D fluid layer at rest; every grid point starts as a dot (zero velocity). A finger moving at speed `v` drags the fluid beside it, so each `0.7` grid spacing of travel leaves a pair of opposite vortex-sheet elements `±Γ` on either side of the path, with `Γ = 0.55 · v · step`. This is the shear layer that bounds a jet. Strength therefore follows finger speed and is independent of the pointer sample rate. Each arrow is the velocity that the vorticity induces through the regularised Biot–Savart law: `u(x) = Σ Γᵢ ẑ × (x − xᵢ) / 2π(|x − xᵢ|² + δᵢ²)`. Untouched regions change too. The same velocity carries the vortex blobs (the 2D Euler equations in vortex-blob form, midpoint integration), so the field reorganises itself after release. A stroke's head rolls up into a dipole that travels on. Crossing strokes wind into each other. A dipole that reaches an edge splits and slides along it, because each edge acts as an approximate free-slip wall: a tapered mirror image for each blob within 0.3 of the short side. Corners and repeated reflections are not modelled. Core spreading (`ν = 10 px²/s`) stands in for viscosity, and a floor-friction rate (`0.05 /s`) slowly returns the layer to rest. A 600 px/s skate still shows about 120 px/s after 10 s and comes to rest after about 75 s. References: Cottet & Koumoutsakos, *Vortex Methods* (Cambridge, 2000), and Krasny, “Desingularization of periodic vortex sheet roll-up”, *J. Comput. Phys.* 65 (1986), for the δ-regularised kernel.

Arrow length shows local speed with a saturating `1 − e^(−|u|/180)` mapping, and speeds under 2 px/s draw as dots. Same-cell blobs are merged above 360 blobs. The merge conserves circulation, and opposite signs cancel. While a finger is down, the layer steps every animation frame. After release, autonomous evolution is capped at 24 Hz, and reduced-motion preferences hold the field still. Canvas DPR is capped at 1.5, like the other options. An inactive option does not animate. One step, including the wall images and 1,150 sampled arrows, takes about 1.5 ms at 250 blobs on the development machine. Phone frame timing has not been measured. Model tests (`model/vortex.test.mjs`) cover: start at rest; jet with side return; nonlocal change; speed-dependent strength; dipole self-propulsion and return to rest; wall containment and splitting; circulation-conserving remeshing; and resize.

- **Baseline:** opt 2's grid, arrow primitive, and pointer discipline.
- **Changed variable:** the finger deposits vorticity, a source of the field, instead of target vectors; the arrows are derived from it globally.
- **Unresolved:** whether the 24 Hz cap reads as smooth on a phone, and whether coupling `0.55` feels proportionate under a real finger. Browser interaction is unverified pending explicit runtime authorization.
